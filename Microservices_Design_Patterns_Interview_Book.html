Part 1 · Foundations & Decomposition

Microservices Fundamentals & Principles

Key Knowledge

Microservices architecture structures an application as a suite of small, independently deployable services, each built around a business capability, owning its data, and communicating over lightweight protocols.

Core Points

  • Single responsibility per service, aligned to bounded contexts (DDD)
  • Independent deployment, scaling and technology choice
  • Decentralized data and governance
  • Design for failure; automate everything (CI/CD, infra as code)
  • Trade-off: operational and distributed-system complexity

Q1. What is a microservices architecture?

An architectural style where an application is split into small, autonomous services, each focused on one business capability, independently deployable, and communicating via APIs or messages.

↑ Contents4

Microservices Fundamentals & Principles · continued

Q2. What is a design pattern in the microservices context?

A reusable, proven solution to a recurring problem in distributed systems, such as service discovery, resilience, data consistency or deployment.

Q3. How do microservices differ from a monolith?

A monolith is deployed as one unit with shared codebase and database; microservices are separately deployed, loosely coupled services with their own data stores.

Q4. What are the main benefits of microservices?

Independent scaling and deployment, technology flexibility, fault isolation, faster team autonomy, and easier adoption of new technologies.

Q5. What are the main drawbacks?

Distributed complexity, network latency, eventual consistency, harder testing and debugging, higher operational overhead, and need for strong DevOps maturity.

Q6. What is a bounded context?

A Domain-Driven Design concept defining a boundary within which a domain model and its terms are consistent; it is the primary guide for service boundaries.

↑ Contents5

Microservices Fundamentals & Principles · continued

Q7. What does "loose coupling, high cohesion" mean for services?

Services should depend minimally on each other's internals (loose coupling) while keeping related behavior together in one service (high cohesion).

Q8. What is the Single Responsibility Principle for microservices?

Each service should have one reason to change and own one business capability.

Q9. What is the "You build it, you run it" principle?

The team that develops a service is also responsible for operating it in production, promoting accountability and quality.

Q10. What is Conway's Law and why does it matter?

Systems mirror the communication structures of the organizations building them; so team structure should be designed to match the desired service architecture.

Q11. What is the Twelve-Factor App methodology?

A set of 12 practices (config in environment, stateless processes, disposability, logs as streams, etc.) for building portable, scalable cloud-native services.

↑ Contents6

Microservices Fundamentals & Principles · continued

Q12. When should you NOT use microservices?

For small teams, simple domains, early-stage products with unclear boundaries, or when the organization lacks DevOps and automation maturity.

Q13. What is a "modular monolith"?

A monolith organized into well-isolated modules with clear boundaries, offering many design benefits of microservices without distributed-system costs.

Q14. What is polyglot persistence and polyglot programming?

Using different databases or languages per service, choosing the best tool for each service's needs.

Q15. What are the eight fallacies of distributed computing relevant here?

Assuming the network is reliable, latency is zero, bandwidth is infinite, the network is secure, topology doesn't change, there is one administrator, transport cost is zero, and the network is homogeneous.

Part 1 · Foundations & Decomposition

Service Decomposition Patterns

Key Knowledge

Decomposition patterns define how to carve a system into services. The goal is stable boundaries that minimize cross-service chatter and align with the business.

Core Points

  • Decompose by business capability
  • Decompose by subdomain (DDD)
  • Self-contained service
  • Service per team
  • Strangler Fig for incremental extraction

Q16. What is Decompose by Business Capability?

Defining services around what the business does (e.g., Order Management, Billing), since capabilities are relatively stable over time.

Q17. What is Decompose by Subdomain?

Using DDD to split the domain into core, supporting and generic subdomains, with each mapped to one or more services.

↑ Contents8

Service Decomposition Patterns · continued

Q18. What is a Self-Contained Service pattern?

A service that can handle a request without synchronously waiting for other services, improving availability and latency.

Q19. What is the Service per Team pattern?

Each service is owned by exactly one team, giving clear accountability and reducing coordination.

Q20. How do you decide the right size for a microservice?

Size it by cohesion and ownership rather than lines of code: a team can understand, build and rewrite it quickly and it changes for one reason.

Q21. What is a nanoservice anti-pattern?

Services so fine-grained that overhead of communication and deployment outweighs benefits.

Q22. How do you identify service boundaries?

Use event storming, bounded contexts, data ownership, change frequency, and transactional boundaries.

↑ Contents9

Service Decomposition Patterns · continued

Q23. What is Event Storming?

A collaborative workshop technique where domain experts map domain events, commands and aggregates to discover boundaries.

Q24. What is an Aggregate in DDD and how does it relate to services?

A cluster of entities treated as a single consistency unit; aggregates should not span services.

Q25. How do you handle shared code or libraries between services?

Minimize sharing; use versioned libraries only for cross-cutting technical concerns, never for domain logic, to avoid coupling.

Q26. What is the difference between orchestration and choreography at the design level?

Orchestration uses a central coordinator to direct workflow; choreography lets services react to events without central control.

Q27. What is a "distributed monolith"?

A system split into services that are so tightly coupled they must be deployed and changed together, giving the downsides of both styles.

Part 2 · Communication & Integration

Communication Patterns

Key Knowledge

Services communicate synchronously (REST, gRPC) or asynchronously (messaging, events). The choice affects coupling, latency and availability.

Core Points

  • Synchronous: REST, gRPC, GraphQL
  • Asynchronous: message queues, pub/sub, event streaming
  • Prefer async for decoupling and resilience
  • Use remote procedure invocation sparingly in call chains
  • Version APIs and keep backward compatibility

Q28. What is Remote Procedure Invocation?

A pattern where a client calls a service synchronously using a protocol such as REST or gRPC and waits for the response.

Q29. What is the Messaging pattern?

Services communicate asynchronously by exchanging messages via a broker or channel, decoupling sender and receiver in time.

↑ Contents12

Communication Patterns · continued

Q30. What is the Domain-Specific Protocol pattern?

Using a protocol suited to the domain, e.g., SMTP for email or MQTT for IoT, instead of a generic one.

Q31. REST vs gRPC: when to choose which?

REST is simple, ubiquitous and good for public APIs; gRPC uses HTTP/2 and Protobuf for efficient, strongly-typed, streaming internal communication.

Q32. When would you choose GraphQL?

When clients need flexible queries that aggregate data from multiple services, reducing over-fetching and under-fetching.

Q33. Synchronous vs asynchronous communication trade-offs?

Sync is simpler and gives immediate responses but couples availability; async improves resilience and decoupling but adds complexity and eventual consistency.

Q34. What is Publish/Subscribe?

A model where publishers emit messages to topics and any number of subscribers receive them independently.

↑ Contents13

Communication Patterns · continued

Q35. What is a message broker?

Middleware such as RabbitMQ, Kafka or ActiveMQ that routes, stores and delivers messages between services.

Q36. Queue vs topic?

A queue delivers each message to one consumer (competing consumers); a topic delivers copies to all subscribers.

Q37. What is the Competing Consumers pattern?

Multiple consumer instances read from the same queue to process messages in parallel for scalability.

Q38. What is the Request/Reply pattern over messaging?

A requester sends a message with a correlation ID and reply-to address; the responder sends the answer to that address.

Q39. What is a correlation ID?

A unique identifier propagated across calls and messages to link related operations for tracing and request matching.

Q40. How do you version service APIs?

Use URI, header or content negotiation versioning, additive changes, and deprecation policies to maintain backward compatibility.

↑ Contents14

Communication Patterns · continued

Q41. What is Consumer-Driven Contract design?

Consumers declare the expectations they have of a provider API, and the provider verifies against them to avoid breaking changes.

Q42. What is the Backward Compatibility rule, "Tolerant Reader"?

Consumers should ignore unknown fields and only depend on what they need, so providers can evolve safely.

Part 2 · Communication & Integration

API Gateway & Backend for Frontend

Key Knowledge

An API Gateway is the single entry point for clients, handling routing, aggregation and cross-cutting concerns. BFF tailors gateways to specific client types.

Core Points

  • Routing, aggregation, protocol translation
  • Authentication, rate limiting, caching at the edge
  • BFF per client type (web, mobile, partners)
  • Avoid putting business logic in the gateway
  • Examples: Kong, NGINX, Spring Cloud Gateway, AWS API Gateway, Azure APIM

Q43. What is the API Gateway pattern?

A single entry point that routes client requests to backend services and handles cross-cutting concerns like auth, throttling and logging.

Q44. What responsibilities does an API Gateway typically have?

Request routing, composition, protocol translation, authentication, rate limiting, caching, monitoring and TLS termination.

↑ Contents16

API Gateway & Backend for Frontend · continued

Q45. What is API Composition at the gateway?

The gateway calls multiple services and combines their results into one response to reduce client round trips.

Q46. What is the Backend for Frontend (BFF) pattern?

A dedicated gateway per client type (web, mobile) that shapes APIs to each client's needs.

Q47. Why use BFF instead of one general gateway?

Different clients have different payload, performance and security needs; separate BFFs prevent a bloated, compromise-heavy shared gateway.

Q48. What are the risks of an API Gateway?

It can become a single point of failure, a performance bottleneck, and a monolithic "god" component if business logic leaks in.

Q49. How do you make an API Gateway highly available?

Run multiple instances behind a load balancer, deploy across zones, and keep it stateless.

↑ Contents17

API Gateway & Backend for Frontend · continued

Q50. API Gateway vs Load Balancer?

A load balancer distributes traffic at L4/L7 without understanding APIs; a gateway adds API-aware routing, auth, transformation and policies.

Q51. API Gateway vs Service Mesh?

Gateways manage north-south (client-to-service) traffic; service meshes manage east-west (service-to-service) traffic.

Q52. How does rate limiting work at the gateway?

It restricts requests per client/key within a time window using algorithms like token bucket or sliding window, returning HTTP 429 when exceeded.

Q53. What is the Gateway Offloading pattern?

Moving shared functionality such as SSL termination, auth or compression into the gateway so services stay simple.

Q54. What is Gateway Routing?

Routing requests to different services or versions based on path, header, host or other attributes.

Part 2 · Communication & Integration

Service Discovery & Load Balancing

Key Knowledge

In dynamic environments service instances change address constantly. Discovery lets clients find instances; load balancing spreads traffic across them.

Core Points

  • Client-side vs server-side discovery
  • Service registry with health checks (Consul, Eureka, etcd, Kubernetes DNS)
  • Self-registration vs third-party registration
  • Load balancing algorithms: round robin, least connections, consistent hashing

Q55. What is Service Discovery?

A mechanism by which services find the network locations of other services' instances dynamically.

Q56. What is a Service Registry?

A database of available service instances and their locations that is kept current via registration and health checks.

↑ Contents20

Service Discovery & Load Balancing · continued

Q57. Client-side discovery explained?

The client queries the registry and picks an instance itself using a load-balancing algorithm (e.g., Netflix Eureka with Ribbon).

Q58. Server-side discovery explained?

The client calls a router or load balancer, which queries the registry and forwards the request (e.g., AWS ELB, Kubernetes Service).

Q59. Self-registration vs third-party registration?

In self-registration the service registers itself; in third-party registration an agent or platform registers it on its behalf.

Q60. How does Kubernetes handle service discovery?

Through Service objects with stable virtual IPs and cluster DNS names, backed by endpoints updated automatically.

Q61. What are common load-balancing algorithms?

Round robin, weighted round robin, least connections, IP hash, and consistent hashing.

↑ Contents21

Service Discovery & Load Balancing · continued

Q62. What is a health check and why is it needed in discovery?

A probe that verifies instance liveness or readiness so unhealthy instances are removed from the registry or rotation.

Q63. What is the difference between liveness and readiness probes?

Liveness determines if a container should be restarted; readiness determines if it can receive traffic.

Q64. What is DNS-based discovery and its limitations?

Using DNS records to resolve services; limited by TTL caching, which can delay updates and make failover slower.

Part 3 · Data & Transactions

Data Management Patterns

Key Knowledge

Data ownership is central to microservices. Each service owns its data and exposes it only through APIs or events, trading strict consistency for autonomy.

Core Points

  • Database per service
  • Shared database (anti-pattern in most cases)
  • CQRS for separating reads and writes
  • Event Sourcing for audit and rebuildable state
  • API composition and materialized views for queries

Q65. What is the Database per Service pattern?

Each service has its own private database that no other service accesses directly, ensuring loose coupling.

Q66. What is the Shared Database pattern and its drawbacks?

Several services use one database; it simplifies joins and transactions but creates tight coupling, schema contention and deployment dependencies.

↑ Contents24

Data Management Patterns · continued

Q67. Database per service: schema-per-service vs server-per-service?

Private tables or schema in a shared server is cheaper; separate servers give stronger isolation and independent scaling.

Q68. How do you query data across multiple services?

Use API Composition or CQRS with a denormalized read model populated by events.

Q69. What is the API Composition pattern for queries?

A composer calls each service owning part of the data and joins the results in memory.

Q70. What are the limits of API Composition?

Inefficient for large joins, higher latency, reduced availability, and lacks transactional consistency across data.

Q71. What is CQRS?

Command Query Responsibility Segregation: separate models for writing (commands) and reading (queries), often with separate stores.

↑ Contents25

Data Management Patterns · continued

Q72. What are the benefits of CQRS?

Independently optimized and scaled read/write sides, simpler queries, and support for multiple read views.

Q73. What are the drawbacks of CQRS?

Added complexity, eventual consistency between read and write models, and data synchronization overhead.

Q74. What is Event Sourcing?

Persisting state as an append-only sequence of domain events; current state is rebuilt by replaying events.

Q75. Benefits of Event Sourcing?

Complete audit trail, temporal queries, ability to rebuild projections, and natural fit for event-driven systems.

Q76. Challenges of Event Sourcing?

Event schema evolution, learning curve, querying complexity, and long replay times without snapshots.

Q77. What are snapshots in Event Sourcing?

Periodic saved states of an aggregate so replay can start from the snapshot instead of from the first event.

↑ Contents26

Data Management Patterns · continued

Q78. Do CQRS and Event Sourcing need each other?

No, they are independent, though they pair well because events can feed read models.

Q79. What is eventual consistency?

A model where replicas or services may be temporarily out of sync but converge to a consistent state if no new updates occur.

Q80. What is a materialized view pattern?

Precomputed, queryable data built from one or more sources, typically updated via events, to speed up reads.

Q81. How do you handle reference data shared among services?

Replicate a local read-only copy via events or caching, or fetch it from an owning service.

Q82. How can you share data without sharing a database?

Via APIs, events carrying state changes, or data replication into consumer-owned stores.

Q83. What is Change Data Capture (CDC)?

Capturing database changes from the transaction log and publishing them as events (e.g., Debezium) for downstream systems.

Part 3 · Data & Transactions

Saga & Distributed Transactions

Key Knowledge

Two-phase commit doesn't scale in microservices. A Saga manages data consistency across services using a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions.

Core Points

  • Choreography-based saga: events drive steps
  • Orchestration-based saga: coordinator directs steps
  • Compensating transactions undo completed steps
  • Design for idempotency and isolation anomalies
  • Alternatives: TCC, outbox, eventual consistency

Q84. What is the Saga pattern?

A sequence of local transactions, each publishing an event or message that triggers the next, with compensations to undo previous steps on failure.

Q85. Why not use two-phase commit (2PC) in microservices?

2PC is blocking, reduces availability, needs coordinator support from all resources, and doesn't suit NoSQL or message brokers.

↑ Contents28

Saga & Distributed Transactions · continued

Q86. What is a compensating transaction?

An action that semantically reverses the effect of a previously committed local transaction.

Q87. Choreography-based saga explained?

Each service listens for events and emits new ones, with no central controller.

Q88. Orchestration-based saga explained?

A saga orchestrator tells participants which local transaction to execute and handles replies and compensation.

Q89. Choreography vs orchestration trade-offs?

Choreography is simple and decoupled for few steps but hard to follow; orchestration centralizes logic and visibility but adds a coordinating component.

Q90. What are the pitfalls of choreography?

Cyclic dependencies, difficulty tracking workflow state, and scattered business logic.

Q91. What are the pitfalls of orchestration?

Risk of orchestrator becoming a smart "god" service and being a potential point of failure if not made resilient.

↑ Contents29

Saga & Distributed Transactions · continued

Q92. What are the saga's isolation limitations?

Sagas lack ACID isolation, so anomalies like lost updates, dirty reads and non-repeatable reads can occur.

Q93. What are countermeasures for saga isolation issues?

Semantic locks, commutative updates, pessimistic view, re-read value, version files, and by-value strategies.

Q94. What are semantic locks?

Application-level flags (e.g., PENDING state) marking records as being modified by a saga, controlling access by other sagas.

Q95. Compensatable, pivot and retriable transactions?

Compensatable steps can be undone; the pivot is the go/no-go point; retriable steps follow the pivot and are guaranteed to eventually succeed.

Q96. How do you handle a failed compensation?

Retry with backoff, make compensation idempotent, use dead-letter queues, and escalate to manual intervention if necessary.

↑ Contents30

Saga & Distributed Transactions · continued

Q97. What is the TCC (Try-Confirm-Cancel) pattern?

A reservation-based protocol: try reserves resources, confirm commits, cancel releases them.

Q98. Give an example of a saga.

Order creation: create order (pending) → reserve credit → reserve inventory → approve order; if credit fails, reject order and release reserved items.

Part 4 · Resilience & Messaging

Resilience & Fault Tolerance Patterns

Key Knowledge

Distributed systems fail partially. Resilience patterns contain failures and keep the system responsive.

Core Points

  • Circuit Breaker, Retry with backoff, Timeout
  • Bulkhead isolation, Rate limiter
  • Fallback and graceful degradation
  • Health checks, Chaos engineering
  • Libraries: Resilience4j, Polly, Hystrix (legacy), Istio

Q99. What is the Circuit Breaker pattern?

A proxy that monitors failures to a remote call and trips open to fail fast when a threshold is exceeded, preventing cascading failures.

Q100. What are the circuit breaker states?

Closed (calls pass), Open (calls fail immediately), and Half-Open (limited trial calls to test recovery).

↑ Contents32

Resilience & Fault Tolerance Patterns · continued

Q101. How does a circuit breaker decide to open?

When failure rate or slow-call rate exceeds a configured threshold over a sliding window of calls or time.

Q102. What is the Retry pattern?

Re-attempting a failed operation, usually for transient faults, with limits on attempts.

Q103. Why use exponential backoff with jitter?

Backoff spaces out retries and jitter randomizes them to avoid synchronized retry storms.

Q104. When should you NOT retry?

For non-idempotent operations without safeguards, permanent errors (4xx) or when downstream is overloaded.

Q105. What is the Timeout pattern?

Setting maximum wait time for a call so threads and resources are not blocked indefinitely.

Q106. What is the Bulkhead pattern?

Isolating resources (thread pools, connections) per dependency so failure in one doesn't exhaust resources for others.

↑ Contents33

Resilience & Fault Tolerance Patterns · continued

Q107. Thread-pool vs semaphore bulkhead?

Thread-pool isolates with dedicated threads and queues; semaphore limits concurrent calls with less overhead.

Q108. What is the Fallback pattern?

Providing an alternative response, such as cached data or default values, when the primary call fails.

Q109. What is graceful degradation?

Continuing to offer reduced functionality when some components fail rather than failing completely.

Q110. What is the Rate Limiter pattern?

Limiting the number of requests in a given period to protect services from overload.

Q111. What is load shedding?

Deliberately rejecting low-priority requests when the system is saturated to preserve capacity for critical ones.

Q112. What is backpressure?

A mechanism where downstream signals upstream to slow down, preventing buffers from overflowing.

↑ Contents34

Resilience & Fault Tolerance Patterns · continued

Q113. What is a cascading failure?

A failure in one service that propagates, such as through exhausted threads or retries, to bring down dependent services.

Q114. What is the Idempotency pattern?

Designing operations so repeated execution yields the same result, enabling safe retries.

Q115. How do you implement idempotency in an API?

Clients send an idempotency key; the server stores results by key and returns the saved result on duplicates.

Q116. What is Chaos Engineering?

Deliberately injecting failures (e.g., Chaos Monkey) in production-like environments to verify resilience.

Part 4 · Resilience & Messaging

Messaging & Event-Driven Patterns

Key Knowledge

Event-driven architecture uses events to propagate state changes, enabling loose coupling and scalability, but requires careful handling of delivery guarantees and ordering.

Core Points

  • Transactional Outbox and Inbox
  • Idempotent Consumer, Dead Letter Queue
  • Event notification vs event-carried state transfer
  • Delivery semantics: at-most-once, at-least-once, exactly-once
  • Kafka, RabbitMQ, SNS/SQS, Azure Service Bus, Pulsar

Q117. What is the Transactional Outbox pattern?

Writing the business change and an outgoing message to an outbox table in the same local transaction; a relay later publishes it to the broker.

Q118. What problem does the Outbox solve?

The dual-write problem, where updating the database and publishing a message can't be done atomically.

↑ Contents36

Messaging & Event-Driven Patterns · continued

Q119. How is the outbox relayed to the broker?

Via a polling publisher or by CDC tailing the transaction log.

Q120. What is the Idempotent Consumer pattern?

A consumer that records processed message IDs so duplicate deliveries do not cause repeated side effects.

Q121. What is the Inbox pattern?

Storing incoming message IDs in a table within the processing transaction to deduplicate and guarantee once-effective processing.

Q122. What is a Dead Letter Queue (DLQ)?

A queue holding messages that couldn't be processed after retries, for inspection and reprocessing.

Q123. Explain delivery semantics.

At-most-once may lose messages; at-least-once may duplicate; exactly-once effects are achieved via idempotency or transactional processing.

↑ Contents37

Messaging & Event-Driven Patterns · continued

Q124. Is exactly-once delivery really possible?

End-to-end exactly-once is generally achieved as effectively-once through at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing; Kafka offers transactional exactly-once within its ecosystem.

Q125. How do you maintain message ordering?

Use partition keys (Kafka) or message groups so related messages go to the same partition/consumer.

Q126. What is event notification vs event-carried state transfer?

Notification events carry minimal data prompting consumers to fetch details; state-transfer events carry the needed data, reducing callbacks.

Q127. What is a domain event?

A record of something meaningful that happened in the domain, named in past tense (e.g., OrderPlaced).

Q128. What is the difference between events and commands?

Events state facts that happened and can have many listeners; commands request an action and target a specific handler.

↑ Contents38

Messaging & Event-Driven Patterns · continued

Q129. What is Kafka consumer group?

A set of consumers sharing partitions of a topic so each partition is read by one member, allowing scaling.

Q130. What is the Claim Check pattern?

Storing large payloads in external storage and passing only a reference in the message.

Q131. What is the Polling Publisher vs Transaction Log Tailing?

Polling periodically queries the outbox table; log tailing reads database changes from the log for lower latency and load.

Q132. What is the Event Bus or Event Backbone?

Shared messaging infrastructure used to publish and subscribe to events across services.

Part 5 · Deployment, Observability & Security

Deployment & Infrastructure Patterns

Key Knowledge

Deployment patterns package and release services reliably. Containers and orchestration provide the foundation; progressive delivery lowers release risk.

Core Points

  • Service per container, orchestration with Kubernetes
  • Sidecar, Ambassador, Adapter
  • Service Mesh for traffic management
  • Blue-Green, Canary, Rolling deployments
  • Serverless as a deployment option

Q133. What is the Service-per-Container pattern?

Packaging each service as its own container image for consistent, isolated, portable deployment.

Q134. What is the Service-per-VM pattern?

Deploying each service instance in its own virtual machine; strong isolation but heavier and slower than containers.

↑ Contents40

Deployment & Infrastructure Patterns · continued

Q135. What is the Serverless Deployment pattern?

Running services as functions managed by a cloud platform, scaling automatically and billing by use.

Q136. What is the Sidecar pattern?

Deploying a helper container alongside the main service in the same pod to provide supporting features such as logging, proxying or security.

Q137. What is the Ambassador pattern?

A sidecar acting as an out-of-process proxy for outbound calls, handling retries, routing and monitoring.

Q138. What is the Adapter pattern in deployment?

A sidecar that normalizes the interface of a service, e.g., exposing metrics in a standard format.

Q139. What is a Service Mesh?

An infrastructure layer of sidecar proxies and a control plane managing service-to-service traffic, security and telemetry (Istio, Linkerd, Consul).

↑ Contents41

Deployment & Infrastructure Patterns · continued

Q140. What does a service mesh provide?

mTLS, traffic splitting, retries, circuit breaking, observability and policy enforcement without code changes.

Q141. What is a Blue-Green deployment?

Running two identical environments and switching traffic from the old (blue) to the new (green) for instant rollout and rollback.

Q142. What is a Canary release?

Routing a small share of traffic to a new version, monitoring, then gradually increasing it.

Q143. What is a Rolling update?

Gradually replacing old instances with new ones so some capacity stays available throughout.

Q144. What are feature flags/toggles?

Runtime switches that enable or disable functionality without redeploying, decoupling deployment from release.

Q145. What is Infrastructure as Code (IaC)?

Managing infrastructure via versioned declarative code (Terraform, Pulumi, CloudFormation) for repeatability.

↑ Contents42

Deployment & Infrastructure Patterns · continued

Q146. What is GitOps?

Using Git as the single source of truth for declarative infrastructure and application state, reconciled automatically (Argo CD, Flux).

Q147. What is the Immutable Infrastructure principle?

Never modify running servers; replace them with new versions built from images.

Part 5 · Deployment, Observability & Security

Observability Patterns

Key Knowledge

Observability lets you understand system behavior from outputs: logs, metrics and traces, plus health information.

Core Points

  • Log aggregation with structured logs
  • Distributed tracing (OpenTelemetry, Jaeger, Zipkin)
  • Application metrics and dashboards (Prometheus, Grafana)
  • Health check API, Audit logging
  • SLI/SLO/SLA and alerting

Q148. What is the Log Aggregation pattern?

Centralizing logs from all services into a searchable store (ELK, Loki, Splunk).

Q149. What is structured logging?

Emitting logs in a machine-parseable format such as JSON with consistent fields like trace ID and service name.

Q150. What is Distributed Tracing?

Tracking a request as it flows across services through spans linked by a trace ID.

↑ Contents44

Observability Patterns · continued

Q151. What are a trace, span and context propagation?

A trace is the end-to-end request; spans are timed operations within it; context propagation passes trace IDs across service boundaries.

Q152. What is OpenTelemetry?

A vendor-neutral standard and set of SDKs for generating and exporting traces, metrics and logs.

Q153. What is the Application Metrics pattern?

Services expose numeric measurements (latency, throughput, errors) collected for monitoring and alerting.

Q154. What are the RED and USE methods?

RED: Rate, Errors, Duration for services; USE: Utilization, Saturation, Errors for resources.

Q155. What are the four golden signals?

Latency, traffic, errors and saturation.

Q156. What is the Health Check API pattern?

An endpoint reporting a service's health, used by orchestrators and load balancers.

↑ Contents45

Observability Patterns · continued

Q157. What is the Audit Logging pattern?

Recording user and system actions in tamper-evident logs for security and compliance.

Q158. What are SLI, SLO and SLA?

SLI is a measured indicator, SLO a target for it, and SLA a contractual commitment with consequences.

Q159. What is an error budget?

The allowed amount of unreliability implied by an SLO, used to balance feature releases and stability.

Part 5 · Deployment, Observability & Security

Security Patterns

Key Knowledge

Microservices enlarge the attack surface, so security must be layered: authenticate at the edge, authorize close to the data, and protect service-to-service traffic.

Core Points

  • Access Token (JWT) propagation with OAuth 2.0 / OpenID Connect
  • mTLS for service identity
  • Zero Trust and least privilege
  • Secrets management (Vault, KMS)
  • API Gateway as security perimeter, not the only one

Q160. What is the Access Token pattern?

Clients obtain a token from an authorization server and present it with each request so services can authenticate and authorize.

Q161. What is JWT and what does it contain?

JSON Web Token: signed claims in header.payload.signature form, carrying identity and permissions.

↑ Contents48

Security Patterns · continued

Q162. OAuth 2.0 vs OpenID Connect?

OAuth 2.0 handles delegated authorization; OpenID Connect adds an identity layer for authentication.

Q163. Where should authentication and authorization occur?

Authenticate at the gateway or identity provider; enforce authorization in each service because it knows the resource context.

Q164. What is mTLS?

Mutual TLS, where both client and server present certificates to authenticate each other and encrypt traffic.

Q165. What is the Zero Trust model?

Never trust implicitly based on network location; verify every request and enforce least privilege.

Q166. What is the Token Relay / propagation pattern?

Passing the user's access token downstream, or exchanging it, to preserve identity across service calls.

Q167. What is the Token Exchange / On-Behalf-Of flow?

A service swaps the incoming token for a new token scoped for a downstream service.

↑ Contents49

Security Patterns · continued

Q168. How do you manage secrets in microservices?

Store them in a secrets manager (Vault, AWS Secrets Manager), inject at runtime, rotate regularly, never commit to code.

Q169. What is the Secure Service-to-Service pattern?

Combining mTLS, service identities (SPIFFE/SPIRE) and authorization policies to restrict who may call what.

Q170. What is token revocation challenge with JWT?

Stateless JWTs remain valid until expiry, so use short lifetimes, refresh tokens, and revocation lists when immediate invalidation is needed.

Q171. What is the OWASP API Security Top 10 relevance?

It lists the key API risks, such as broken object-level authorization and excessive data exposure, which should guide design and testing.

Part 6 · Quality, Migration & Scenarios

Configuration & Testing Patterns

Key Knowledge

Externalized configuration keeps builds environment-agnostic, while a layered test strategy (heavy on unit and contract tests) keeps feedback fast without costly end-to-end suites.

Core Points

  • Externalized configuration & config server
  • Consumer-driven contract tests (Pact)
  • Test pyramid: unit > integration > contract > e2e
  • Service virtualization, test containers
  • Testing in production with observability

Q172. What is the Externalized Configuration pattern?

Keeping configuration outside the deployable artifact, supplied at runtime via environment, files or a config service.

Q173. What is a Configuration Server?

A central service (Spring Cloud Config, Consul, etcd) providing versioned config to services, often with dynamic refresh.

↑ Contents52

Configuration & Testing Patterns · continued

Q174. How do you handle configuration changes without restart?

Use dynamic refresh mechanisms, watches on config stores, or feature flags.

Q175. What is the test pyramid for microservices?

Many fast unit tests, fewer integration and contract tests, and a small number of end-to-end tests.

Q176. What is contract testing?

Verifying that a provider and consumer agree on API interactions without running both together (e.g., Pact, Spring Cloud Contract).

Q177. Why avoid heavy end-to-end tests?

They are slow, flaky and hard to maintain; contract and component tests give faster, more reliable feedback.

Q178. What is component testing of a service?

Testing a service in isolation with its dependencies stubbed or containerized.

Q179. What is service virtualization / stubs and mocks?

Simulating dependencies' behavior so tests don't need the real services.

↑ Contents53

Configuration & Testing Patterns · continued

Q180. What are Testcontainers?

A library that spins up disposable Docker containers (databases, brokers) for realistic integration tests.

Q181. What is testing in production?

Using canaries, shadow traffic and feature flags with monitoring to validate behavior under real conditions.

Q182. What is Shadow Traffic / Traffic Mirroring?

Copying live requests to a new version without affecting users, to compare behavior.

Q183. How do you test event-driven flows?

Use in-memory or containerized brokers, verify published events via contracts, and use idempotency and replay tests.

Part 6 · Quality, Migration & Scenarios

Migration & Refactoring Patterns

Key Knowledge

Moving from a monolith should be incremental, delivering value early and limiting risk.

Core Points

  • Strangler Fig pattern
  • Anti-Corruption Layer
  • Branch by Abstraction
  • Parallel run and shadowing
  • Extract the least coupled capability first

Q184. What is the Strangler Fig pattern?

Gradually replacing monolith functionality with new services behind a routing facade until the legacy system can be retired.

Q185. What is the Anti-Corruption Layer (ACL)?

A translation layer that shields a new model from a legacy or external model's concepts.

Q186. What is Branch by Abstraction?

Introducing an abstraction over a component, building a replacement behind it, and switching over incrementally.

↑ Contents56

Migration & Refactoring Patterns · continued

Q187. How do you choose the first service to extract?

Pick a capability with clear boundaries, low coupling, high business value or high change/scale pressure.

Q188. How do you split a shared database?

Identify data ownership, separate schemas, replace joins with API calls or replicated data, and move tables gradually.

Q189. What is a Parallel Run?

Running old and new implementations side by side and comparing outputs before cutover.

Q190. What is the Expand and Contract pattern (Parallel Change)?

Add new structures alongside old, migrate consumers, then remove the old ones.

Q191. How do you keep data in sync during migration?

Using CDC, dual-writes with reconciliation, or event streams until ownership is fully transferred.

Q192. What is the Facade pattern in migration?

A single interface placed in front of legacy and new systems to route traffic and hide the transition.

↑ Contents57

Migration & Refactoring Patterns · continued

Q193. What are common migration mistakes?

Big-bang rewrites, extracting too many services at once, ignoring data separation, and underinvesting in automation and observability.

Part 6 · Quality, Migration & Scenarios

Anti-Patterns & Scenario Questions

Key Knowledge

Knowing what to avoid and how to apply patterns to real problems distinguishes experienced candidates.

Core Points

  • Distributed monolith, chatty services, shared DB
  • God service / smart orchestrator
  • Lack of observability and versioning
  • Choose patterns by requirement, not fashion

Q194. What are common microservices anti-patterns?

Distributed monolith, shared database, nanoservices, chatty communication, god services, missing observability, and no API versioning.

Q195. How would you design an e-commerce checkout across services?

Use an orchestrated saga across Order, Payment, Inventory and Shipping services, with outbox messaging, idempotent consumers and compensation for failures.

↑ Contents60

Anti-Patterns & Scenario Questions · continued

Q196. A downstream service is slow and taking the whole system down. What do you do?

Add timeouts, circuit breaker, bulkheads and fallbacks, then investigate with tracing and apply load shedding or scaling.

Q197. How would you ensure that a message is not lost or duplicated when updating a DB and publishing an event?

Use the Transactional Outbox with at-least-once delivery and idempotent consumers.

Q198. How do you handle a reporting need across many services?

Build a read model via CQRS fed by events or CDC into a dedicated analytics store.

Q199. How would you roll out a risky change safely?

Use feature flags, canary release with automated metric checks, and fast rollback.

Q200. How would you debug a slow request spanning ten services?

Use distributed tracing to find the slow span, then correlate with metrics and logs for that service.
